Skew —
the targz language as hardware

If the targz language were rebuilt in the hardcore skeuomorphic register — anodised chassis, backlit keys, jewel LEDs, engraved plates, edge-lit acrylic, knurled knobs — what happens to the things we actually ship? Six of them, redrawn: the layer dock, the nav bar, the page switcher, the layer editor, the viewport and a Launchpad card.

Skew, because it is the word the hand types when it means skeuomorphic, and because that is also the whole trick: a flat plane tilted into depth, lit from one lamp above left, and nothing here is actually raised.

Every anatomy is the real one — the layer dock is lifted control-for-control from Portrait-Typo/ui.js. Two rules held throughout: every button is a real button with a body, a skirt and travel, momentary or latching; and every slider that sets a quantity became a knob, except where the value is a length, which stays a fader. Everything on this page works — press it.

This is a separate document. index.html — the design system — is untouched, and none of these tokens exist there. Treat this as a look-see: the panel below is live, everything clicks, and the last two sections are the honest bill for adopting it.

Left, the physical build — every control works: the switches latch, the masters read on / mixed / off, the chevrons open the stroke strip, add and delete change the list, and the footer counts what it will destroy. Right, what ships today, at the same scale.

02 / The nav bar · momentary vs latching

The one idea flat UI cannot express. Import, Export and Console are momentary — they spring back and pulse once, because they DID something. Reference, Light and Cells are latching — they stay down with the lamp on, because they ARE something. Today all six are the same grey icon and the difference lives in a tooltip. Press them.

momentary · latching · readout

03 / The page switcher · mechanical interlock

Three latching keys on one bar, and the bar holds one down at a time — the segmented control is finally the thing it has always been drawing. The key that is down is down: 4px lower than its neighbours, lit from inside, with a lamp bar under the engraving.

3-position interlock

04 / The layer editor · knobs instead of sliders

L1's Place tab. Every slider that sets a quantity became a knob: 270° of travel, a lit arc for the value and a pointer you can read at a glance. Drag up or right, or use the wheel; hold shift for fine. A knob is one 48px param strip — knob, engraved label, readout on a single line — instead of the caption / knob / readout stack, which spent three rows and about 100px on one control. Zone from / Zone to stayed faders on purpose: that pair is a length, and a length wants a straight track — so they were built as real ones, with a cut slot, an engraved scale and a waisted cap carrying the indicator line. The Place from dropdown became a rotary selector — one pip per position ringed round the dial, dark until the pointer lands on it, so you can count the choices without reading them.

rotary · 270° · shift = fine

05 / The viewport · gizmo, keypad, three knobs

The axis ball sits in a well under a glass dome — its six nodes are the six view keys, so you can hit a view from the picture or from the pad. Then Yaw / Pitch / Roll as knobs, which is what those three always wanted to be: three angles you dial, with the pointer showing the angle literally.

Pan / Turn / Select moved out. Everything in that panel answers where the camera is; those three answer what the mouse does, which stays true while you are not looking at the viewport at all. Two questions, two objects — so they get a tool palette of their own, narrow enough to sit anywhere, and separate enough to be moved.

axis ball · 6-key pad · 3 × rotary

06 / Launchpad · one rack module per project

The dashboard card as a rack unit. The green running bar is a light pipe, the status dot is a pilot lamp that is dark until the process is actually alive, the port is a lit display, and Launch / Stop are illuminated pushbuttons with the lens inside the cap. Launch one and the whole module powers up — rail, lamp, display, and the running count at the top.

07 / The hatch bay · a compound control

Five bare rows — Spacing, Pen, Min stroke, Join reach, Colour — every one the same shape with a green number at the end, so nothing on screen said which one you turn to change how the drawing looks. Hatching is the one place where the parameters are physical objects, so the bay is built round a specimen: a lit sphere on its own shadow, drawn at 1:1 mm on paper with real spacing, real stroke, real angle and real ink. It is a ball rather than a swatch because a rectangle of even hatch shows the spacing and hides everything else — and what hatching is for is tone: the same pen and the same angle making light and dark by how much paper it leaves. Everything else here is an input to that one picture.

The type is a selector switch — parallel lifts the pen at the end of every pass, serpentine does not and is one continuous stroke, crossed is a second pass at 90°, hand hatched breaks the register so the fill has no direction you can read. Four positions on one dial, all visible at once with the lever on the one you are on. They are four different paths, not four textures, which is why the specimen draws geometry and clips it to the sheet: a serpentine is its turnarounds, and on an over-sized grid every turnaround falls off the paper.

Then three knobs, because this page's own rule says a quantity is a knob and only a length keeps a track — and pen, spacing and angle are three single quantities. Pen runs free from 0.1 to 3 mm: what is being set is a stroke width, and a stroke width is a quantity you dial until it looks right against the specimen in front of you. Spacing is a distance in millimetres and angle detents at 0 / 45 / 90 / 135. The three are independent — no control here quietly edits another, so a value you set by hand stays the value you set. Take spacing under the nib and the strokes overlap into solid ink: not a fault, and not reported as one. It is a fill, it is something you might want, and the specimen shows it the way it shows everything else. Colour is a cap whose face is the ink: the case ships with black, grey and white and six empty slots, and every one of them can be refilled — a swatch you cannot change is a swatch lying about being a pen. Click a full one twice, or an empty one once, and it opens the picker: a plate made of the same metal as everything else. The two cleanup thresholds are in a recess below the fold, because they are set once and never touched again.

tonal specimen · 1:1 mm · 4 types · 3 knobs

08 / Filling · by hand, and by machine

One section, two halves, and they want opposite things from the same job. Above: three fills that must not look like they came off a machine. Below: the fill only a machine can draw. They share a rig — the same ball, the same lamp, the same blob with the same hole in it — because that is the only way the two arguments can be read against each other.

The bay's four types are machine fills and say so: a plotter draws paths, and parallel / crossed / serpentine are three paths. This is the opposite job — a fill that comes off the same machine and does not look like it. Its Hand hatched position is not the answer, which is why these three are new work and not a tuning of it: breaking the register makes a fill look noisy, and noisy is not the same thing as hand-made.

What a hand actually does is a short list, and every item is a thing a loop does not do. It does not space evenly — an unvarying pitch is a louder tell than straightness ever was. It does not come back at the same angle. It does not stop on the line: a stroke runs past the boundary or pulls up short, and never by the same amount twice. It re-grips, so past about 16mm there are no edge-to-edge strokes at all. And it builds tone by going back over — four passes, each confined to what is already darker than the last, which is why every threshold is dithered. A clean one draws a contour map of the lighting, and that is the one thing that never happens by hand.

Two specimens each, and neither is optional. The ball is the tone test: the one shape whose correct answer everybody already knows, so a hand that cannot make it round is caught on sight — form-following strokes, the terminator darker than the silhouette, the table's bounce light lifting the lower rim, and a cast shadow that is the only thing saying "table". The shape carries no shading at all, one density edge to edge, because a circle has no corner and no neck: a fill can be perfect on a ball and come apart the first time it meets a real outline.

It is geometry, in millimetres, at 1:1. No filter, no blur, no fill anywhere in the block — every mark is a stroked path at the nib's own width, so what is on screen is what a pen puts on paper and the same paths go out to an SVG and onto the plotter with no rescale. That is the only condition under which a specimen is worth looking at.

The second half, and it wants the opposite thing. The three above want a fill nobody can tell came off a machine; this one wants the fill only a machine can draw. No wobble, no jitter, no drift — exact pitch, straight lines, the machine's side taken completely.

What it optimises is not the picture, it is the pen lift. A plotter's real cost is not line length: the head moves at the same speed whether the pen is down or up. It is the number of times the pen has to come out of the paper, aim, and come back down — and every one of those is a chance to blot, to miss by a tenth, and to leave the little comma a felt tip makes when it lands. A parallel fill pays that once per pass.

3D printers solved this first, and the answer is the same in two dimensions: at the end of a pass, do not lift — turn. Run the next pass backwards so its start is where the last one ended, and the two are one stroke with a corner in it. Across a whole region, a fill that was two hundred strokes is one. The turnaround is the entire visible difference, which is why the bay clips its lines to the sheet: on an over-sized grid every turnaround falls off the paper and a serpentine renders as a parallel fill with a different name on the dial.

Shading is where it gets interesting. A uniform region is easy — the passes are full chords, they all touch, one stroke does the lot. Carve those same passes by tone and the region breaks into islands, and now the question is whether the pen may travel from one to the next. It may, if the gap is short enough, and if the line it would draw on the way stays inside the region it is filling. Skip that second test and the fill grows whiskers across the white paper between its islands, which is worse than the lift it saved. The first number is Join reach, already sitting in the hatch bay's set-once recess — this is what it does.

And then it stops, deliberately. There is a version of this that gets the flat shape down to one stroke: route the pen along the outline where the straight cut runs out, and finish by carrying on round the perimeter. It works, the readout says 1, it was built, and it is gone — because the count is not the drawing. Every one of those tricks buys a lift with a line, and a line is permanent: the wall gets inked twice where the pen retraces it, and the last join reaches from wherever the fill happened to stop to wherever the perimeter happened to start, which is a stroke straight across the middle of the shape that nothing asked for. One of those ruins a plot. Sixty pen lifts do not. So the rule here is the conservative one — turn where the next pass is genuinely next to you, lift otherwise. A serpentine is a fill that does not lift when it does not have to, not a fill that never lifts, and chasing the second is how you draw the first line you cannot erase.

So the section is a control and a result. Left column lifts at the end of every pass. Right column turns instead, and joins across islands wherever the reach allows — and nowhere else. Same tone model, same pitch, same nib, same drawing — and a lit readout under each saying how many times the pen went down. That number is the only argument being made here.

And it proves the first half's claim for free. Nothing down here is dithered — a machine has no hand, so there is nothing to disagree with — and the four thresholds are therefore visible as bands on the ball, each one a clean edge where a pass starts. That is the contour map of the lighting that the hands above spend a paragraph avoiding. Both balls are correct; only one of them looks drawn.

09 / Materials

These primitives carry all six components. Every control on this page is one of them at a size — which is what makes it a language rather than six hand-drawn pictures. The count is written by the tiles themselves, because the sentence said nine for three versions after the tenth was built.

10 / The bill

What it buys, and what it costs. Both lists are about this component specifically, not about skeuomorphism in general.

What it genuinely buys

  • The colour bar becomes a light source. Edge-lit, it spills onto the metal beside it — so a layer's ink is visible in peripheral vision, which a 3px flat bar never was. This is the one improvement I would actually miss.
  • On/off stops being a colour comparison. A lit lamp against a dead one is a brightness difference, readable without comparing to the row next door and readable if you cannot separate the two oranges.
  • Off-state honesty. A hidden layer has a dead pipe and a raised cap — the row reads as unpowered from across the screen, where today it is a 0.3 opacity dim you have to look for.
  • Destructive controls can be guarded materially — recessed instead of raised, hazard stripes on hover — instead of relying on red alone.
  • Momentary vs latching is finally sayable. A key that springs back did something; a key held down by the mechanism is something. The nav bar has both kinds and today they are the same grey icon.
  • Knobs beat sliders for angles. Yaw / Pitch / Roll as three pointers is the one substitution that is not a preference — the control's angle is the value, so you read three numbers without reading any.

What it costs

  • It is dark-only. Every effect here is light modelling against a dark body. There is no light-mode version of this panel — and the apps' canvas is paper-white, so the panel would sit on the drawing like a foreign object.
  • ~14 shadows per row. The flat row is one background and one bar. Five of these on a scrolling, dragging list is real paint cost, and drag-reorder with glow + blur is where it will show.
  • Text contrast fights the texture. 9.5px summaries on brushed metal are already marginal here; the shipping row spends its width on a name and two numbers, and grain eats small type first.
  • It out-shouts the drawing. The whole point of the current chrome is that it disappears next to the plot. This does not disappear.
  • Nothing else in the system speaks it. Tweakpane, the nav bar, the tab bar and the log would all need rebuilding, and Tweakpane is explicitly "do not hand-build this" in the design system. Section 04 is a hand-built Tweakpane, which is the rule this exploration breaks hardest.
  • Knobs are worse than sliders at three things, and they matter: no drag-anywhere-along-the-line (you must grab the cap), no slam-to-the-end, and they are poor on touch. Every knob here needs a number box beside it to be usable for typing a value — which is half a slider back.
  • Every knob is a pointer-drag handler. Tweakpane gives that away free, with keyboard, touch and value entry already solved and tested.
